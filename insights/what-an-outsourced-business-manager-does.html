What an outsourced business manager does, and how to tell if you need one

Every small firm has a job that nobody was hired for. It is the work around the work, and it usually lands on the owner at nine at night. An outsourced business manager exists to take it.

The job nobody was hired for

Think about how a small firm is staffed. There are the people who do the work the client pays for. There may be someone at the front desk, and someone outside who does the books. That is usually everyone.

Now think about what the firm needs done in a month. The website has to be kept current. Enquiries have to be answered and followed up. Reviews need replies. The newsletter is due. A vendor has sent the wrong thing. The profile on the industry directory is out of date. Somebody should write up the project that finished in March. Somebody should call the local paper about the anniversary.

None of that belongs to anyone. So it belongs to the owner, who is also the firm’s best fee earner. The owner does it late, in pieces, or not at all.

A large company solves this with managers and departments. A small firm cannot afford a department and does not have enough of any one task to fill a salaried role. What it has is ten hours a week of important, unowned work spread across six different skills.

That is the gap an outsourced business manager fills.

A plain definition

An outsourced business manager is a person or small team from outside your firm who takes ongoing responsibility for the business tasks around your core work. They work to an agreed list, in a regular rhythm, as if they were on your staff.

Three words in that definition matter.

Ongoing. A consultant studies a problem, writes a report and leaves. A business manager is still there next month, doing the thing the report recommended.

Responsibility. An assistant does what they are told. A business manager owns an outcome. If the reviews have not been answered, that is their problem to notice and fix, not yours to remember.

Agreed. The list is written down. You know what you are getting. They know what is theirs. Anything new is discussed before it is added.

What it is not

The title gets confused with several others, so it helps to draw the lines.

  • Not a bookkeeper or an accountant. They keep your records and file your returns. A business manager works alongside them and makes sure they get what they ask for on time.
  • Not a virtual assistant. An assistant handles tasks you delegate one at a time. Useful, and different. You are still the manager.
  • Not a marketing agency. An agency sells a marketing service and usually a specific one. A business manager looks at the firm as a whole and treats marketing as one part of running it. We have written a fuller comparison.
  • Not a consultant. Advice has its place. This is execution.
  • Not a replacement for you. Your judgment, your relationships and your name stay yours. A good business manager protects your time for exactly those things.

One caution about the term. In entertainment, a business manager is specifically a financial professional who looks after a performer’s money. That is a separate, specialized kind of work. Here we mean the general sense: someone who manages the business side of a small firm.

What a week looks like

The clearest way to understand the role is to watch one week.

Monday. A standing conversation with the owner. Twenty or thirty minutes. What happened last week, what is due this week, what needs a decision. The manager writes it up in five lines and sends it round.

Tuesday. Follow-up. Three enquiries came in over the weekend. Each gets a proper reply and a next step. A quote from last week that went quiet gets a polite nudge.

Wednesday. Content. The month’s social posts and one article are drafted from notes the owner dictated on the drive home. They go to the one person who approves things.

Thursday. Loose ends. The web developer is chased about the broken form. The directory listing is corrected. A new review gets a reply drafted. The photographer is booked for the finished project.

Friday. Approved pieces are scheduled. A short note goes to the owner: what went out, what came in, what is waiting on them.

Nothing in that week is dramatic. That is the point. A firm’s reputation is built from small things done on time, every week, by someone whose job it is.

Why marketing belongs in the job

Many firms treat marketing as a separate purchase. They hire someone to build a website, someone else for social media, and perhaps a publicist for a launch. Each does their piece. Nobody connects them, and nobody is there when the project ends.

In a small firm, marketing is less a campaign than a set of habits. The website stays current because someone updates it when a project finishes. Reviews get answered because someone checks each week. The newsletter goes out because it is on a calendar with a name beside it. These are management tasks. They fail for management reasons: no owner, no deadline, no follow-up.

That is why we put marketing, PR and social media inside the business manager’s job. The person who knows what the firm did this month is the right person to tell the world about it. And the reason most owners fall behind on marketing is that they are buried in other work, so helping with only one of the two never quite works.

Signs you need one

You do not need a business manager because your firm is small. You need one if several of these are true.

  • You are the best person in the firm at the paid work, and you spend a day a week on things that are not the paid work.
  • Your website describes the firm as it was two or three years ago.
  • Enquiries sometimes wait days for a reply, and you know some were lost.
  • You have started and abandoned a newsletter or a social account more than once.
  • You have paid for marketing that produced a report and no change.
  • There are finished projects, good results or real news that the outside world has never heard about.
  • You are about to raise money, open a location or launch something, and the thought of the preparation makes you tired.
  • When you take a week off, the business side stops.

Signs you do not

Honesty cuts both ways. An outsourced business manager is probably the wrong answer if:

  • You have a capable office or operations manager with time to spare. Give them the list and the authority first.
  • The firm’s real problem is the service itself. No amount of management or marketing fixes work that clients are unhappy with.
  • You want someone to take instructions task by task. An assistant will serve you better and cost less.
  • You are not willing to let anyone else write in the firm’s name, even with your approval. The arrangement depends on trust, and it cannot work without some.
  • You need one specialized thing done at scale, such as a large advertising program. That is an agency’s job.

What to ask before you hire one

If you are talking to a firm like ours, or to anyone offering this kind of help, these questions will tell you most of what you need to know.

  1. What exactly will I receive, and by when? Ask for the list in writing. Vague scope is where disappointment starts.
  2. Who does the work? You should know the name of the person you will speak to each week.
  3. Who owns the accounts, the content and the contact list? The only acceptable answer is that you do.
  4. How do approvals work? There should be one approver on your side and a clear time limit, so work does not stall.
  5. What do you not do? A good answer is specific. Be wary of anyone who does everything.
  6. What will you not promise? Rankings, press coverage and client numbers are outside anyone’s control. Someone who guarantees them is telling you what you want to hear.
  7. How do we stop? It should be simple, and you should leave with everything that is yours.

What it should cost you in time

The fee varies with the scope. The time is more predictable. A working arrangement should need about an hour a week from the owner: a short standing conversation and a batch of approvals. If it needs much more, the manager is not managing. If it needs none, they are not close enough to the firm to represent it well.

Expect the first month to ask more of you. Someone new has to learn how you talk, what you care about and where things are kept. After that the hour buys back most of a day.

Questions we hear

Is an outsourced business manager the same as a fractional COO?

They overlap. A fractional chief operating officer usually works at a more senior level on structure, hiring and systems. A business manager, as we use the term, is closer to the ground: the weekly tasks, the follow-up and the firm’s marketing.

How many hours a week does a business manager work for one firm?

It depends on the agreed list, not on a number of hours. We prefer to define what you receive and when, so you are paying for results you can see.

Can a business manager work remotely?

Yes. Most of the work is done by phone, shared documents and your own online accounts. We are in Santa Monica and work with firms across the country.

Will my clients know someone outside the firm is involved?

Only if you want them to. Everything goes out in the firm’s name, after your approval.
